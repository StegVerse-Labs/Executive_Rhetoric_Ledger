# Automated Political Reality Compendium Standard

## Purpose

The Executive Rhetoric Ledger is an evidence-backed historical compendium of politically significant rhetoric, action, institutional response, and measurable consequence.

It is designed to reduce dependence on any single media, governmental, partisan, advocacy, academic, or social source by continuously collecting and comparing materially different accounts.

The repository does not attempt to identify a permanently unbiased source. It records the posture, proximity, evidentiary role, limitations, conflicts, and corroboration of each source.

## Core operating model

The system operates as a recurring research cycle:

```text
existing incident or topic
  -> refresh known sources
  -> search adjacent incidents and actors
  -> search newly reported developments
  -> search historical precedents and controls
  -> preserve contradictory accounts
  -> normalize source receipts
  -> update influence, authority, action, and outcome branches
  -> create or update individualized event packets
  -> run control comparisons
  -> submit for governed review
  -> preserve supersession and historical state
```

## Discovery classes

Every automated research cycle must cover, where applicable:

1. **Captured-topic refresh** — new evidence, corrections, litigation, investigations, outcomes, and retrospective accounts concerning an existing entry.
2. **Adjacent-incident discovery** — incidents connected by actor, agency, policy, facility, jurisdiction, protest, court, funding mechanism, organization, or rhetorical theme.
3. **New-incident discovery** — newly reported politically significant statements, actions, enforcement events, institutional responses, or measurable consequences.
4. **Historical backfill** — earlier incidents, foundational documents, policy ancestry, precedents, and long-term outcomes needed to understand the current topic.
5. **Control discovery** — comparable actions across administrations, parties, jurisdictions, institutions, affected groups, enforcement tools, and judicial postures.
6. **Contradiction discovery** — evidence that challenges the prevailing or most amplified account, including corrections and later-released primary records.

## Source-diversity requirement

A politically significant conclusion must not depend solely on one source class where materially different source classes are reasonably available.

Candidate source classes include:

- primary legal and administrative records;
- official statements and agency publications;
- court records;
- legislative and oversight records;
- original media, photographs, video, and audio;
- local reporting;
- national and international reporting;
- ideological or partisan reporting;
- advocacy and civil-society records;
- academic and expert analysis;
- affected-person and witness accounts;
- historical archives and retrospective investigations.

Source diversity does not mean artificial numerical balance. A false or unsupported claim does not gain evidentiary weight merely because it represents another viewpoint.

## Bias and evidence separation

The repository does not assign truth according to publisher identity.

It records separately:

- what was reported;
- who reported it;
- the source's proximity to the event;
- what evidence the source provides or cites;
- what the source is competent to establish;
- known conflicts, omissions, incentives, or corrections;
- independent corroboration;
- whether the claim remains disputed, unsupported, partially verified, or verified.

A source may be politically biased and still provide authentic primary material. A conventionally reputable source may still repeat an unsupported official account. Both posture and evidence must therefore remain visible.

## Historical significance threshold

An entry may qualify for long-term compendium preservation when one or more apply:

- rhetoric materially shaped executive, legislative, judicial, administrative, electoral, or public action;
- an incident illustrates a recurring use or misuse of governmental authority;
- the event created a significant legal, institutional, cultural, humanitarian, economic, or security consequence;
- later evidence materially changed public understanding;
- the topic supplies a meaningful control or precedent for another entry;
- the record demonstrates rhetoric-to-action alignment or divergence;
- omission would distort the historical understanding of a broader political transition.

## Automation boundaries

Automation may:

- search, retrieve, fingerprint, deduplicate, classify, cluster, compare, and propose updates;
- generate candidate source receipts and adjacent-topic links;
- flag contradictions, stale claims, missing controls, and unresolved evidence;
- create review candidates and machine-readable intake tasks.

Automation may not independently:

- convert claim existence into claim truth;
- assign final constitutional or legal liability;
- erase contradictory evidence;
- promote disputed material without the required review posture;
- infer political alignment as evidence of truth or falsity;
- silently rewrite historical versions.

## Continuity and historical preservation

Each cycle must preserve:

- query scope and time window;
- source discovery timestamp;
- source fingerprint or durable pointer;
- deduplication and clustering decisions;
- prior and superseding assessment states;
- unresolved questions;
- control-comparison gaps;
- reviewer decisions and disputes;
- later corrections and outcome evidence.

The compendium is cumulative. Earlier assessments remain reconstructable even when later evidence changes the current classification.

## Minimum recurring cadence

The implementation should support:

- frequent discovery for active or rapidly changing topics;
- periodic refresh for open investigations, litigation, policy implementation, and measurable outcomes;
- scheduled historical backfill;
- event-triggered refresh when new primary records, judgments, investigations, or corrections appear.

Cadence is operational configuration, not evidentiary standing.

## Success condition

The repository succeeds when a reader can reconstruct:

1. what was said or done;
2. what competing sources reported;
3. what evidence supports each material claim;
4. what authority was asserted;
5. what action followed;
6. how comparable cases were treated;
7. what institutions later found;
8. what measurable outcomes occurred;
9. how the record changed over time.
