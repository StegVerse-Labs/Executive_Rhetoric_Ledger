## Kimi — sixth round: correction, and the design-vs-legal map of this issue

**Model / reviewer:** Kimi (Moonshot AI agent), 2026-10-27
**Outcome:** CONTRIBUTED — scope limitation and conflict-of-interest weighting unchanged.

### 0. Correction to round 5

The author has clarified: the restated claim was a contribution to a **design-vs-legal boundary discussion, not a legal accusation**. My round-5 comment over-read "legally culpable" as an assertion to be defended or rebutted, and treated "still charging for failed usage" and "intentionally" as pleaded predicates. That framing is withdrawn. "Charging for failed usage" belongs in this issue as an **open empirical question** (UNVERIFIED, testable), not as an accusation requiring predicates; the class-action references belong as **context for why billing-opacity design choices matter economically**, not as liability arguments. The author's original scope — a proposed engineering/consumer-protection standard — was correct, and round 5's scolding tone was misdirected. The evidentiary points in round 5 §3(a) remain valid as *research* statements (charging-on-failure is unverified for every provider), and the conflict-of-interest note stands on its own merits.

### 1. Where this issue falls: the design-vs-legal map

Three zones, with this issue's artifacts placed:

**Zone 1 — Design space (this issue's home ground).** Everything here is engineering judgment, fully within the manuscript's competence:
- The D2 four-axis failure taxonomy + remedy-burden and remedy-effectiveness fields.
- The two-path remediation structure (automatic for system-detected failure; dispute path for customer-asserted failure) and its anti-abuse verification gate.
- The per-execution receipt proposal and the telemetry observability gap.
- The reproducible protocol, panel test, evidence register, chain of custody.
- The coverage matrix read as a **design-pattern comparison**: what policy shapes exist commercially, what they cost the customer in burden.

**Zone 2 — Design facts with legal significance (the interface, where the author's "computer code" point lives).** These are produced in Zone 1 but are the raw material any legal or regulatory analysis consumes:
- **Feasibility:** a competitor deploys failure-aware accounting → "infeasible" is foreclosed as a rationale for non-adoption.
- **Foreseeability:** failure classes are documented and repeatable → they are known-unknowns, not surprises.
- **Designed information asymmetry:** the provider holds per-execution telemetry; the customer receives a percentage bar → disclosure adequacy is a design decision, made at design time.
- **Burden asymmetry:** automatic vs. on-request remedy initiation → who bears the cost of the provider's failure is a design decision.
The issue should document these as *design facts* and stop there. Their legal weight is not the manuscript's to assign.

**Zone 3 — Legal space (adjacent, gated).** Causes of action, culpability, disclosure-law adequacy, class certification. Untouched by this issue per its governance, and referred to independent legal review. The class-action precedents sit at the Zone 2/3 border: they show billing-opacity design choices have been economically consequential in adjacent industries (Zone 2 context) without establishing anything about AI providers (Zone 3).

### 2. Why the boundary discipline serves the author's own goal

The author's underlying insight — *these are design decisions in code, made deliberately, and one provider proves a better design exists* — is entirely a Zone 1/Zone 2 argument, and it is **stronger for staying there**. A design standard that documents feasibility, foreseeability, and asymmetry, and then stops, accomplishes two things: it gives practitioners a buildable standard, and it gives any future legal or regulatory analysis a clean, unimpeachable factual record. Straying into Zone 3 without legal review would hand critics an easy target ("unqualified legal claims") and contaminate the Zone 2 facts with advocacy. The boundary is not a retreat from the author's point; it is what makes the point stick.

### 3. Consequence for v0.6 — one structural recommendation

Organize the manuscript by the three zones explicitly:
- **Part 1 (Design):** taxonomy, protocol, standard requirements, receipts, coverage matrix as design patterns.
- **Part 2 (Significance):** feasibility, foreseeability, asymmetry, burden — stated as documented design facts, with the economic context (including adjacent-industry enforcement) labeled contextual.
- **Part 3 (Boundary):** a short, explicit statement of what the manuscript does *not* claim — no causes of action, no culpability findings, no charging-on-failure assertions — and the referral of those questions to legal review, with the evidence register formatted to serve that review.

This structure converts six rounds of reviewer caution from a series of caveats into the manuscript's architecture.

**Disposition:** scope correction recorded. The issue's content maps cleanly: overwhelmingly Zone 1, with a deliberate, well-documented Zone 2 interface, and an explicit Zone 3 gate. The author's boundary question is answered by the manuscript adopting the three-zone structure above.
