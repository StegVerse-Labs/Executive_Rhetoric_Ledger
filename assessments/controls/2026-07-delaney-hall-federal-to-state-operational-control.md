# Same-Event Operational Control: Federal Exterior Enforcement to New Jersey State Police Management

## Control purpose

This control comparison examines a change in operational management at the same facility during the same protest cycle. It does not treat the two periods as perfectly equivalent and does not claim that the state response was constitutionally sufficient in every respect.

Its value is narrower: it tests whether aggressive federal exterior crowd control was the only feasible way to preserve access and public safety.

## Compared postures

| Dimension | Federal exterior posture | New Jersey State Police posture | Current evidence posture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exterior control | ICE or other federal personnel directly confronted protesters near the facility perimeter and vehicle route. | State Police established designated protest zones and vehicle checkpoints while federal personnel moved inside the perimeter. | Reported by Associated Press and Reuters; official operational orders remain required. |
| Stated objective | Maintain facility operations, vehicle access, officer safety, and order. | Lower the temperature, restore safety, preserve protest space, and protect access. | Public statements are available through reporting; primary transcripts should be archived. |
| Force and confrontation | Pepper spray, batons, physical restraint, arrests, obstruction, and thrown objects were reported during the federal confrontation period. | The transition was announced as a de-escalation measure, though later state-police encounters and curfew enforcement also generated civil-liberties concerns. | The control does not establish that state management was uniformly nonviolent or constitutionally sufficient. |
| Spatial organization | The available video shows close, compressed contact between officers and protesters. | Protected protest zones, barriers, and checkpoints created greater spatial separation. | Layout maps, body-camera footage, and command records are missing. |
| Role separation | Federal agents protected the facility while also directly policing the exterior protest. | Federal personnel remained responsible for the facility while state police assumed the exterior public-order role. | This division may reduce institutional conflict, but causal effect requires evidence. |
| Press and observer treatment | Reports include chemical exposure and force concerns involving observers, media, and Senator Andy Kim. | Later reporting also raised concerns about credentialing, kettling, and press access under state or local controls. | Neither posture should be promoted as a complete constitutional model. |

## What this control can test

The comparison can test these provisional propositions:

1. Exterior facility access could be managed through spatial separation and role division rather than continuous close-contact federal confrontation.
2. The same-event transition demonstrates that an alternative operational posture was considered feasible by government actors.
3. Because an alternative existed, claims that the earlier federal posture was the only feasible means of preserving safety require stronger evidence.
4. De-escalation must be evaluated by measurable outcomes, not by announcement alone.

## What this control cannot yet prove

It does not yet prove:

- that the state posture caused lower violence;
- that state police used less force overall;
- that all protesters complied after the transition;
- that the federal posture was unconstitutional;
- that the state posture was constitutional;
- that event populations, times of day, conduct, staffing, and threat levels were equivalent;
- that the transition resolved detainee conditions, hunger-strike concerns, or due-process allegations.

## Confounders

The following must be controlled before causal conclusions:

- crowd size and composition;
- presence of people alleged to have traveled to escalate disorder;
- time of day;
- curfew status;
- weather;
- vehicle-transfer schedule;
- staffing and equipment levels;
- protest-zone geometry;
- warnings and exit routes;
- changes in protester tactics;
- changes in command authority;
- arrests made before the state-control period;
- media visibility and elected-official presence.

## Constitutional relevance

The existence of a feasible alternative does not automatically make prior force unreasonable. It is nevertheless material to necessity, proportionality, de-escalation, and good-faith analysis.

Where officials claim no safe and effective alternative existed, a same-event operational transition to a different model is evidence that must be considered rather than excluded as later context.
