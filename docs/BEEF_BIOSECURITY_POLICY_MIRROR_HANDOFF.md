## Goal
Determine, with sourced chronology and explicit causal boundaries, how current-administration policy changes affected U.S. beef supply, cattle availability, animal-disease/parasite exposure risk, surveillance capacity, emergency response spending, and procurement beneficiaries without conflating temporal association with proven causation.

## Current bounded findings
1. U.S. cattle inventory was already historically depleted before the current administration: the January 1, 2025 cattle-and-calves inventory was the lowest since 1951; beef-cow inventory was the lowest since 1961.
2. The May 2025 Mexican live-cattle import suspension tightened feeder-cattle availability in an already constrained domestic market; the administration later acknowledged the restriction exacerbated feedlot supply constraints.
3. The administration froze/terminated substantial foreign-assistance and global-health-security activity in early 2025, including programs supporting international disease surveillance, veterinary capacity, laboratories, and transboundary-animal-disease preparedness.
4. USDA/APHIS also experienced substantial workforce losses, reducing standing animal-health personnel while transboundary disease threats were active.
5. These facts support a systemic-risk question: whether simultaneous weakening of forward surveillance, international information exchange, and domestic animal-health capacity reduced detection/containment margin for diseases or parasites that threaten Americans. They do not establish that those cuts caused New World Screwworm spread.
6. Screwworm is retained as one case study, not as the sole theory: the outbreak advanced north before the administration took office, while later U.S. policy responses included Mexican cattle restrictions and major emergency reinvestment.
7. Emergency-response spending identified so far includes a roughly $105M USDA New World Screwworm Grand Challenge, a roughly $610M Moore Air Base sterile-fly facility construction contract, a sole-source aviation-support stream, and additional sterile-fly/international-response funding.
8. Seven private for-profit Grand Challenge recipients identified in USDA materials have public evidence of operating history predating the 2026 awards by more than two years; this does not resolve beneficial ownership, conflicts, award reasonableness, lobbying, political contributions, subcontracting, or cumulative prior federal funding.
9. The Dynamic Aviation sole-source justification has now been reconstructed. USDA selected Dynamic based on current NWS aerial-dispersal work in Panama/Mexico and existing sterile-fruit-fly dispersal contracts in Florida/California; no sources-sought notice or additional solicitation preceded award; pricing was supported by historical contract data and an Independent Government Cost Estimate; USDA committed to compete follow-on NWS aerial-dispersal work during the initial 6-12 months.
10. The public J&A shows the same named USDA official, Craig Harper, signing as Contracting Officer, Competition Advocate, and Head of Contracting Activity. FAR approval-level requirements appear facially satisfied by HCA approval for an action in the publicly reported ~$44.1M range, but role concentration and USDA delegation/independence remain unresolved governance questions rather than violations established by this record.
11. Dynamic Aviation had verified federal lobbying relationships before the NWS award, including reported $40K quarterly lobbying activity. The disclosed 2025 issues located so far concerned defense/intelligence appropriations and ISR, not USDA, screwworm, or animal health. A second lobbying registration was filed in June 2026 after the NWS contract award. Lobbying is therefore verified, but procurement-targeted lobbying or improper influence is not established.
12. Public records identify other APHIS aerial-dispersal contractors in related sterile-insect work. This does not prove they were viable NWS emergency alternatives; it creates a capability-comparison discriminator for USDA's sole-source determination.
13. Public reporting on the 2025 foreign-assistance terminations identifies roughly $250M in FAO global-health-security / transboundary-animal-disease projects affected, including work involving avian influenza, New World Screwworm in Central America, and swine fever. FAO later confirmed surveillance, laboratory, veterinary-training, and preparedness work was delayed, scaled back, or could not proceed as originally planned.
14. Roughly $72M associated with 16 Feed the Future Innovation Labs was also disrupted; this must not be treated wholly as animal-health funding because the labs covered broader agricultural research. FY2026 funding was later restored at the headline level, but the successor portfolio does not necessarily restore the same labs, staff, geography, relationships, or functions.
15. Partial replacement/rebuilding is now documented: continued USDA bilateral/regional work; later State Department successor global-health-security activity; FY2026 Feed the Future funding; and a new FAO/IAEA five-year NWS research project. The relevant question is the duration, geography, and functional depth of any capability gap—not a claim that all international capacity vanished permanently.

## Promotion boundaries
- No claim that the administration caused screwworm or another pathogen/parasite outbreak without direct causal evidence.
- No corruption, favoritism, profiteering, or improper-steering finding from award receipt, lobbying, political contributions, or role concentration alone.
- No company is classified as illegitimate solely because it is small, privately held, specialized, federally funded, politically active, or represented by lobbyists.
- Chronology, risk amplification, functional-capacity loss, procurement beneficiary analysis, and causal inference must remain separately labeled.
- Restoration of headline funding does not equal restoration of prior personnel, institutional relationships, geographic coverage, or operating capability.

## Recurrence
