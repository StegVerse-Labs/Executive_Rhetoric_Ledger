## Goal
Determine, with sourced chronology and explicit causal boundaries, how current-administration policy changes affected U.S. beef supply, cattle availability, animal-disease/parasite exposure risk, surveillance capacity, emergency response spending, and procurement beneficiaries without conflating temporal association with proven causation.

## Current bounded findings
1. U.S. cattle inventory was already historically depleted before the current administration: the January 1, 2025 cattle-and-calves inventory was the lowest since 1951; beef-cow inventory was the lowest since 1961.
2. The May 2025 Mexican live-cattle import suspension tightened feeder-cattle availability in an already constrained domestic market; the administration later acknowledged the restriction exacerbated feedlot supply constraints.
3. The administration froze/terminated substantial foreign-assistance and global-health-security activity in early 2025, including programs supporting international disease surveillance, veterinary capacity, laboratories, and transboundary-animal-disease preparedness.
4. USDA/APHIS also experienced substantial workforce losses, reducing standing animal-health personnel while transboundary disease threats were active.
5. These facts support a systemic-risk question: whether simultaneous weakening of forward surveillance, international information exchange, and domestic animal-health capacity reduced detection/containment margin for diseases or parasites that threaten Americans. They do not establish that those cuts caused New World Screwworm spread.
6. Screwworm is retained as one case study, not as the sole theory: the outbreak advanced north before the administration took office, while later U.S. policy responses included Mexican cattle restrictions and major emergency reinvestment.
7. Emergency-response spending identified so far includes a roughly $105M USDA New World Screwworm Grand Challenge, a major Moore Air Base sterile-fly facility construction program, a sole-source aviation-support stream, international surveillance/control, and additional sterile-fly/international-response funding.
8. Seven private for-profit Grand Challenge recipients identified in USDA materials have public evidence of operating history predating the 2026 awards by more than two years; this does not resolve beneficial ownership, conflicts, award reasonableness, lobbying, political contributions, subcontracting, or cumulative prior federal funding.
9. The Dynamic Aviation sole-source justification has now been reconstructed. USDA selected Dynamic based on current NWS aerial-dispersal work in Panama/Mexico and existing sterile-fruit-fly dispersal contracts in Florida/California; no sources-sought notice or additional solicitation preceded award; pricing was supported by historical contract data and an Independent Government Cost Estimate; USDA committed to compete follow-on NWS aerial-dispersal work during the initial 6-12 months.
10. The public J&A shows the same named USDA official, Craig Harper, signing as Contracting Officer, Competition Advocate, and Head of Contracting Activity. FAR approval-level requirements appear facially satisfied by HCA approval for an action in the publicly reported ~$44.1M range, but role concentration and USDA delegation/independence remain unresolved governance questions rather than violations established by this record.
11. Dynamic Aviation had verified federal lobbying relationships before the NWS award, including reported $40K quarterly lobbying activity. The disclosed 2025 issues located so far concerned defense/intelligence appropriations and ISR, not USDA, screwworm, or animal health. A second lobbying registration was filed in June 2026 after the NWS contract award. Lobbying is therefore verified, but procurement-targeted lobbying or improper influence is not established.
12. Public records identify other APHIS aerial-dispersal contractors in related sterile-insect work. This does not prove they were viable NWS emergency alternatives; it creates a capability-comparison discriminator for USDA's sole-source determination.
13. Public reporting on the 2025 foreign-assistance terminations identifies roughly $250M in FAO global-health-security / transboundary-animal-disease projects affected, including work involving avian influenza, New World Screwworm in Central America, and swine fever. FAO later confirmed surveillance, laboratory, veterinary-training, and preparedness work was delayed, scaled back, or could not proceed as originally planned.
14. Roughly $72M associated with 16 Feed the Future Innovation Labs was also disrupted; this must not be treated wholly as animal-health funding because the labs covered broader agricultural research. FY2026 funding was later restored at the headline level, but the successor portfolio does not necessarily restore the same labs, staff, geography, relationships, or functions.
15. Partial replacement/rebuilding is documented: continued USDA bilateral/regional work; later State Department successor global-health-security activity; FY2026 Feed the Future funding; and a new FAO/IAEA five-year NWS research project. The relevant question is the duration, geography, and functional depth of any capability gap—not a claim that all international capacity vanished permanently.
16. CRS R49124 (2026-08-11), using USDA/APHIS data supplied in May 2026, reports approximately $999.234M in NWS emergency-transfer obligations from FY2024 through May FY2026. Major components include $726.406M for Moore Air Base production-facility construction, $162.196M for sterile-insect production/release, $47.363M for international outreach/surveillance/control, $35.239M for sterile-insect facilities in Mexico, and smaller research/operations/dispersal-center categories. These are obligations, not necessarily completed expenditures.
17. The CRS $726.406M Moore Air Base construction obligation materially exceeds the public $610M Mortenson design-build contract headline. The difference is unresolved and must be reconciled before treating either figure as complete project cost or contractor benefit.
18. CRS also establishes a longstanding baseline: APHIS used about $20M annually since FY2012 for Panama sterile-insect production/field activity, with about $28M annually before FY2012 when two production facilities were supported for part of the period. Emergency spending therefore must be compared against an existing containment baseline, not against zero.
19. CRS preserves important contrary evidence on barrier failure: neither USDA, foreign governments, nor WOAH had publicly determined why the Panama biological barrier failed; possible contributing conditions cited by USDA include pre-2025 cattle-population growth in Darién, producer wound-care/education issues, and sterile-fly-production input supply/quality constraints. 2025 policy cannot be assigned as the cause of the 2023 barrier breach.
20. The current outbreak reached the United States on 2026-06-03. CRS reports 45 confirmed U.S. detections by 2026-08-10 (44 Texas, 1 New Mexico). Canada has since imposed NWS-related restrictions on susceptible animals from Texas and additional certification requirements for other U.S. exports, creating a new measurable trade-impact surface.
21. APHIS lists Douglas, Arizona for phased reopening to Mexican cattle imports on 2026-08-24; Santa Teresa and Columbus, New Mexico remain to be determined. This creates a live test of how USDA balances cattle-supply constraints against biosecurity risk.
22. AcceleRAD Technologies, selected for Grand Challenge project `APP-113469`, was acquired by Reveam, Inc. on 2026-03-19, before USDA announced selections in June. This does not undermine AcceleRAD's technical legitimacy, but it changes the economic-beneficiary/ownership path and requires the actual notice of award, parent disclosure, and ownership/financing review.
23. Moore Air Base is not an active Air Force installation for this project. USDA ARS records identify the relevant site as `APHIS-VS property known as Moore Air Base` and describe 222.943 acres of APHIS property there. USACE Fort Worth District is serving as USDA's design/construction agent, and Mortenson is the construction contractor for the public $610M project. This narrows the $726.406M-vs-$610M reconciliation: acquisition of the Moore site should not be presumed to explain the difference, and the remaining accounting should be decomposed across the prime award/modifications, USACE agent costs, enabling/site work, government-furnished equipment, engineering/support, commissioning, contingency/reserve treatment, and any separate obligations booked to the construction category. No waste, overpayment, profit, or misconduct finding follows from the difference alone.

## Promotion boundaries
- No claim that the administration caused screwworm or another pathogen/parasite outbreak without direct causal evidence.
- No corruption, favoritism, profiteering, waste, overpayment, or improper-steering finding from award receipt, lobbying, political contributions, role concentration, acquisition/ownership change, sole-source status, or an unreconciled obligation/contract-value difference alone.
- No company is classified as illegitimate solely because it is small, privately held, specialized, federally funded, politically active, represented by lobbyists, or acquired by another company.
- Chronology, risk amplification, functional-capacity loss, procurement beneficiary analysis, and causal inference must remain separately labeled.
- Restoration of headline funding does not equal restoration of prior personnel, institutional relationships, geographic coverage, or operating capability.
- Federal obligations do not equal completed expenditures or contractor profit.
- A total program obligation does not equal a single prime-contractor award value.

## Recurrence
Research is active and future-changing. Periodic source acquisition and ERL updates are required when materially new evidence changes chronology, funding totals, recipient ownership/status, procurement competition, trade restrictions/reopenings, restoration status, causal confidence, or policy interpretation.
