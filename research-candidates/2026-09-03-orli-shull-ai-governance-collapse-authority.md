## ERL research question

When does an AI-generated observation, classification, prediction, or probabilistic interpretation become an authoritative institutional state, and what governance properties are required to prevent inference, identity, authority, and consequence from collapsing into a single unreviewable transition?

## Candidate significance

This candidate is relevant to ERL because it isolates a transition-boundary problem rather than merely an accuracy problem:

`observation -> inference -> authoritative state -> consequence`

The research frontier is whether governance can formally preserve distinctions among:

- observed evidence;
- inferred or probabilistic interpretation;
- unresolved alternatives and unknown classes;
- declared authority;
- admissible transition conditions;
- reversible versus irreversible consequence;
- human challenge/contextualization rights;
- temporal opportunity to intervene before consequence;
- identity claims versus task-local classifications.

The candidate also intersects the StegVerse Humans-as-Interoperability-Layer (HIL) research direction. That relationship is a research linkage only; it does not establish that the source author endorses StegVerse, HIL, or any StegVerse architecture.

## Competing hypotheses / discriminators

### H1 — Human interruption is the principal missing governance control

Expected signature: systems with meaningful human interruption authority materially reduce harmful or unjustified inference-to-consequence transitions even when the underlying model remains probabilistic.

### H2 — Human-in-the-loop is insufficient without structural transition governance

Expected signature: human review does not reliably prevent collapse when reviewers lack explicit authority, provenance, uncertainty representation, temporal slack, reversible state transitions, or access to alternatives/disconfirming evidence.

### H3 — The key failure is premature epistemic collapse

Expected signature: material harms correlate with systems converting uncertain candidate interpretations into durable records, identity labels, eligibility states, or other authoritative conclusions before evidence supports such finalization.

### H4 — The key failure is authority assignment rather than probability itself

Expected signature: probabilistic inference is not inherently problematic until a system, institution, or actor grants the inference authority to produce consequence without an independently governed transition.

### H5 — Identity-sensitive contexts require stronger non-finalization guarantees

Expected signature: systems used in employment, benefits, criminal justice, healthcare, education, credit, security, or other identity-affecting domains require materially stronger challenge, provenance, reversibility, and uncertainty controls than low-consequence classification systems.

## Disconfirming / limiting evidence to seek

- institutional examples where probabilistic models produce authoritative decisions but robust appeal/reversal mechanisms prevent durable harm;
- domains where automatic finalization outperforms human review without unacceptable consequence;
- evidence that added human review creates delay, bias, inconsistency, or false confidence rather than improved governance;
- formal systems that preserve uncertainty without human intervention;
- cases where identity finalization is necessary, bounded, temporary, and independently reviewable;
- empirical evidence separating model uncertainty from institutional authority failure.
